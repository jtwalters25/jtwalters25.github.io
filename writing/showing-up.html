Showing Up: What Fatherhood Taught Me About Time, Experiences and Leadership

Capacity is fixed. Presence is a choice.

By Jeremiah Walters · November 2026

I'm a software engineer and team lead in reliability engineering. I'm also an active father in a busy, blended household. It's easy to treat those as two separate jobs competing for the same hours.

They're less separate than they look.

The skills that keep my home running are the same ones that make me a better lead: managing time I can't make more of, designing experiences people remember and leading through clarity instead of control. Each side keeps making me better at the other.

One caveat first. My family isn't a team I manage, and my kids aren't a backlog. But the patterns rhyme, and paying attention to them has changed how I work.

Time: Capacity Is Fixed, So Priorities Have to Be Real

Engineers know you can't fix a capacity problem by wishing. A system has the throughput it has. Fatherhood taught me to treat my own time the same way.

The calendar is the source of truth. If something matters, it's on the calendar, whether it's a design review, a game or a pickup. If it isn't on the calendar, it's a hope, not a plan.

Limit work in progress, and promises in progress. In software, too much work in progress means everything moves slowly and nothing finishes. At home, half-kept promises do the same damage: "we'll do that soon" piles up until it means nothing. I'd rather commit to fewer things and finish them.

Plan the week before it starts. A short planning session up front saves hours of scrambling midweek, at home and at work.

Plan for degraded mode. In reliability work, we decide in advance what a system should shed first when it's overloaded. I do the same with my week. When a sick kid and a production issue land on the same day, I already know what gives and what doesn't.

Automate the repeats. At work, I look for repeated tasks to template or automate. At home, the equivalent is turning recurring decisions into routines. If we have the same coordination conversation every week, it should be a system, not a debate.

Presence beats attendance. I can't be at everything. Showing up means being fully present where I said I'd be, and honest about where I can't. One game I'm actually watching beats three where I'm half on my phone. The same goes for meetings.

Lesson: You can't add hours. You can only decide, honestly and in advance, where they go.

Experiences: Design the Moments, Not Just the Logistics

Logistics can quietly take over family life. Schedules, rides, meals and homework keep a house running, but nobody remembers a well-run Tuesday. People remember the moments someone created on purpose.

So I try to design experiences deliberately: small, repeatable rituals and the occasional bigger memory. One I'm working to get going is a monthly family art gallery. Everyone makes something on their own, in whatever medium they want, then we hang it around the house and walk through it together, talking about what each person made. With an age range that wide, keeping it monthly is the hard part. That's exactly why it's worth designing.

What I've learned about creating them:

  • Start small and repeatable. A tiny ritual that happens every week beats an elaborate plan that happens once. It's the same instinct as validating a small slice before building the grand vision.
  • Make room for one-on-one time. In a busy house, attention is the scarcest resource, and the loudest need tends to win. Time that belongs to one person, even a short drive, tells them they're seen.
  • Let people shape it. The experiences that stick are the ones people helped create.

It also shapes how I lead. Our industry talks a lot about developer experience: tools, pipelines, documentation. There's also team experience. What does a new teammate's first week feel like? Does a demo feel like a celebration or a test? Does a retro lead to real change? Teams remember those moments the way kids do, and a lead can design them on purpose.

One-on-ones matter at work for the same reason they matter at home. In a group, people say what's safe to say in front of everyone. One on one, they tell you what's actually going on.

Leading: Clarity, Ownership and Consistency

Pointed, not fuzzy. At work, I push for communication that says what's true, what it means and what's needed. Kids deserve the same. "We'll see" is fuzzy, and kids are experts at decoding fuzzy. "Yes, Saturday after practice" or "Not this week, and here's why" is pointed. It's easier to hear, even when the answer is no, and easier for me to keep.

Ownership, not cleanup. If a lead cleans up after everyone, it never ends, and it teaches the wrong lesson. At home, everyone owns their own piece at a level that fits their age. At work, every engineer keeps their own work visible and current. In both places, a predictable rhythm beats nagging.

Agree before you announce. My partner and I lead our household together. When we're aligned before the kids hear a decision, things go smoothly. When we're not, they find the gap immediately. Co-leads at work are no different: disagree in private, decide together and give one answer.

Consistency earns trust. In a blended family, a title doesn't do the work for you. Trust comes from showing up the same way on ordinary days and keeping small promises. The same is true whenever I lead people who didn't choose me. Authority on paper is worth very little. Consistency earns the rest.

Own the misses. Some weeks work wins, and I'm not where I said I'd be. When that happens, I say it plainly, skip the excuses and do better next time. Kids notice whether you own your mistakes. So do teams.

Habits That Work in Both Places

  • Put what matters on the calendar, or admit it's a hope.
  • Commit to fewer things, and finish them.
  • Decide in advance what gives when the week breaks.
  • Design one experience on purpose. Start small.
  • Give each person time that's only theirs.
  • Say pointed things, especially when the answer is no.
  • Agree before you announce, and own your misses fast.

What I Want Them to See

I want my kids to see that you can care deeply about your work without letting it consume you, and that being ambitious and being present aren't opposites. I want my team to see the same thing.

Showing up isn't a grand gesture. It's an ordinary day done well, and then the next one.

I don't always meet that standard. But I know what it is, and I keep showing up.
